# 2026-09-14

## Read to the end, and the threshold that wasn't

`isReadToTheEnd` was `libraryState == .finished || progress >= 0.995`, and the threshold was the whole
of the trouble. A hundredth of a long book is a chapter of a short one: publish one chapter into a book
of a million characters and the reader's progress falls from 1 to 0.997, which still clears 0.995, so
the book goes on reading as one they are done with. That is what put a grey pencil, the mark for waiting
on the author, on a book with a chapter nobody had read.

There are two ways to be done with a book and no third: the reader was shown its last page, or they
filed it as finished themselves. Both were already recorded. `ReaderScreen.Model.bookProgress` weighs
the current chapter by `(currentPage + 1) / pageCount`, so the last page of the last chapter comes to
exactly one and nothing else does, and `markAsRead` writes a whole one and files the book on Finished.
So the rule is `libraryState == .finished || progress >= 1`, and `Book.readThreshold` is gone.

**A whole one has to survive being recomputed.** `SQLiteBookStore.recomputeProgress` derives the
fraction again from the reading position whenever the contents are stored, and a position is the head of
the page the reader is on, never the foot of the chapter. So a book finished at exactly one came back as
0.999 the next time its contents were refreshed, which under the old threshold passed unnoticed and
under this rule would take the book off the shelf. It now leaves a book alone where the reader stands in
the last chapter and the row already says one. A chapter published past them is what moves them off the
end, and it moves this too: their chapter is no longer the last, so the fraction is derived and falls.

That last part is the fix the pencil actually needed. A book gaining a chapter now stops being read to
its end on its own, and the bookmark goes back to saying how far along the reader is. Being told about
unread chapters through `ReadingMark(_:isFresh:)` still earns its place for a book the reader filed as
finished by hand, where no count can unfile it.

One threshold stayed, re-homed. `WorkScreen.Model.chapterRead` marks a chapter read at 0.995 of its own
length, which is a different question with the same shape: the last page of a chapter never reaches its
last character either, and the chapter marks were not what was wrong.

A rule this central was checked twice against suites that never ran: `--unit` covers the packages, and
the app's own tests, `ReadingMarkTests` among them, only run under `--only BookholdTests`.

## A gigabyte of books and a two-and-a-half gigabyte backup

The Profile screen said the device held 1.26 GB of books and that the backup of them ran to 2.6 GB.
Both numbers were wrong in the same direction, and the larger one was nearer the truth.

`downloadSize()` summed `LENGTH(html)` and `LENGTH(content)` over two `TEXT` columns. SQLite's `length()`
counts characters on text and bytes only on a blob, and Cyrillic is two bytes a character in UTF-8:

```
sqlite> SELECT length(html), length(CAST(html AS BLOB)) FROM t;   -- 'Привет мир'
10|19
```

So a library of Russian books was reported at half its size, dressed up as bytes by
`.byteCount(style: .file)`. The backup's own walk was honest about bytes but summed `fileSizeKey`, which
is what a file holds rather than what it takes.

Both now go through `DiskSpace.taken(by:)`: the blocks a file is given, never less than the bytes in it,
because a file kept in the cloud and not downloaded here is allocated nothing and is still that big. The
Books row stopped asking the rows how long they are and asks the file system instead, and it counts all
three places books take room: the library file and its journal, the covers printed off it, and
everything an imported book brought with it. That is the same set the backup holds, so the two numbers
can now be read against each other.

`clearDownloads` already ran `VACUUM`, which is what makes a file-sized figure behave: clearing gives the
pages back and the number falls with them.

The backup stays the larger of the two, and should. It holds a vacuumed copy of the database, so it
carries no free pages, but it is written uncompressed onto whatever drive the reader chose.

## A shelf that could not be true

A reported book stood at 91%, its reading position at the head of the last of thirteen chapters, and its
cover carried the pencil that means the reader is waiting on the author. The backup said why in one row:
`library_state` was `Finished` while `is_finished` was 0, so the book was filed as read through by an
author who was still writing it.

`isReadToTheEnd` reads the shelf before the count, so 0.91 never got a hearing, and `ReadingMark` fell
past its `.reading` branch to the pencil. The count was right and the label was stale.

The fix is the label rather than the rule that reads it. Finished is the reader's word for being done,
and it cannot hold while there are chapters still to come, so it is now refused in the three places it
could arrive:

- **At the door.** A book stored with Finished and an author still writing goes onto Reading instead.
  The service keeps sending its own answer, and the store keeps declining it, which costs nothing and
  never diverges further.
- **When the author writes again.** `store(chapters:)` compares what arrives against what the device
  held, and a chapter it has never seen takes the book off Finished. The first contents a book ever gets
  is exempt, every chapter of it being new.
- **When the reader says so.** `markAsRead` files a book as Finished only where its author has finished
  it. The count still goes to a whole one, which is the honest half of what the reader meant: they have
  read everything published.

Measured over the library in the backup, 68 books sit on Finished. Ten carry a reading position with a
count below a whole one, and six of those are imported books whose counts were scrambled when a
re-import re-cut them. Six more have no position here and sit at 0.999, which is the service's own
rounding on books read elsewhere. That spread is what ruled out the simpler fixes: dropping the shelf
from the rule would have dragged all six of those back onto Reading at 99.9%, and trusting any count
below a whole one would have taken the re-imported books with them.

The correction stays on the device. Nothing is written back to author.today, so a book refiled here is
still Finished there.

## A sheet, and the page that had to stay exactly where it was

The reader was given a spread: two pages side by side where the window has room for two that read. The
whole of it comes down to one substitution. `ChapterLayout.Context.pageSize` used to be the window's
size; now it is one page of `PageSpread`, and everything below that point was left alone. A page is
still measured, drawn and hit-tested in its own coordinates, and nothing under the spread knows there
is more than one of them on screen.

The constraint that shaped the arithmetic is that `pageSize` is part of the layout fingerprint, and the
fingerprint keys both caches: chapter placements and composed columns. Measure the page a single point
differently and every book on every device is re-measured on first launch after the update. So the
spread has to hand a phone in portrait the window size back, to the point, and it does: the sides of
the safe area are zero there, the room is the full width, and one page takes all of it.
`PageSpreadTests.leavesAPhoneExactlyAsItWas` is that promise written down.

Spending the safe area outside the spread rather than in its gutter is what makes two pages come out
identical. Measured with their own leading and trailing insets they would differ by a notch's width in
landscape, and the book would have to be measured twice.

## Width alone is not enough to choose a spread

Width alone chose a spread badly. An iPhone 17 Pro Max on its side is 956 by 440, which halves to two
columns 407 wide against a page 440 deep: room enough by any measure test, and nearly square. Two
almost-square pages of about twenty lines each don't read as a book.

So a page has to keep a page's shape before two of them count as a spread. The first answer was 1.2
times taller than wide, which sorted every device tried without a special case: the small pad in
portrait halves to 360 against 1133 and gets two, a pad given a third of the screen falls back to one.
Wide margins do the same, which is right, since a reader who wants margins that wide wants one page.
The figure itself turned out to be too strict, and what corrected it is further down.

The other ceiling is on the measure itself, 440 points of text. A 13-inch pad in portrait halves to 500
a side, which is a long line, so what it has over is laid outside the spread instead. That's where a
book's widest margins are anyway.

## Sheets either side of a chapter are counted on the neighbour's grid

`PageTurnView` turns sheets and was left untouched, which was the point of the design. What it asks for
is `-1` and `sheetCount` when there is a chapter to cross into, and the first attempt answered those by
carrying on past the ends of this chapter's own pages: sheet `-1` is pages `-2` and `-1`, and so on.

That settles half a page off. A chapter of five pages read two at a time ends on a sheet holding page
four and the first page of the next chapter; the sheet after it would then be that chapter's pages one
and two, while the turn lands the reader on its page zero. The spread the reader was shown slides by one
the moment they let go.

The fix is to count the boundary sheets on the grid of the chapter they belong to rather than on this
one's. `pages(onSheet:)` does it with the remainder of the neighbour's own landing page, which comes out
right for a chapter that runs on as well: where the next chapter starts on the page this one ended on,
those are the same physical page and the two grids already agree.

## Both halves of a wide shelf

A shelf's covers were sized by `round(available / 96)`, so a wider screen made more of them rather than
bigger ones: at 393 points a library stands three covers across at 107 each, and at 1024 it stood ten at
90. Books on a tablet came out smaller than the same books on a phone.

`idealCover(across:)` grows the wanted size with the shelf instead, to a ceiling of 144. A phone is
untouched, since a sixth of its shelf is under the grid size already. Everything downstream was left
alone: spine thickness is a share of the cover width and follows on its own.

## One modifier for a sheet and a popover

The reader's appearance form is a half-height sheet on purpose, so the page behind it can be watched
taking the change. On a tablet a medium detent is a full-width slab across the bottom of a very large
screen.

`.popover` hung on the button that opens it, with `.presentationCompactAdaptation(.sheet)`, gives both
behaviours from one modifier and no size-class branch: a phone still gets the half-height sheet with its
detents, a tablet gets the form beside the button with the book uncovered. The popover's size comes from
`.frame(idealWidth:idealHeight:)`, which a sheet ignores, because a popover is asked how big it wants to
be and a sheet is told.

The tab bar took two lines: `mode = .tabBar` so four tabs are never folded into a sidebar, and
`tabBarMinimizeBehavior` of `.never` where there is room to spare, since the bar only needs to get out
of the way when the shelf it covers is the whole screen.

## A literal with no type of its own compares false

`#expect(one.pageSize.width == 440 + 24 * 2)` failed against a width that Swift Testing printed as
`488.0`, with the other side printed as `488`. The two aren't the same value to the compiler: with no
context forcing it, the right-hand expression takes a type of its own, and the comparison that gets
picked is not the `CGFloat` one. Nothing warns, and the printed failure shows two numbers that read as
equal.

Naming the expected value fixes it, and reads better besides. `private static let widestPage: CGFloat
= 440 + 24 * 2` is typed once where it's declared, and the test then says what it's checking rather
than restating the arithmetic. Worth keeping in mind anywhere a `CGFloat` is compared to a sum of
literals.

## A `didSet` that tidies its own property never comes back

Guarding the column count against zero looked like one line:

```swift
var columns = 1 {
    didSet { columns = max(1, columns) }
}
```

On a plain stored property that terminates, because assigning inside `didSet` doesn't run `didSet`
again. On an `@Observable` class it doesn't. The macro rewrites the property into a computed pair over
a stored `_columns`, and the assignment in the observer goes back through the computed setter, which
runs the observer, which assigns again. The reader took the whole stack down on the first launch that
opened a book: `EXC_BAD_ACCESS` on the stack guard page, with `columns.getter` and `_columns.didset`
alternating all the way up the trace.

Nothing catches this. It builds clean, every unit test passes, and the crash only shows up when a view
actually sets the property. The screenshot on the device is what found it.

The invariant belongs where the value is handed over instead. `columns` is `private(set)`, and the view
passes it to `apply(context:columns:)` alongside the page size, which clamps it once. That pairing is
honest besides: a spread that gained or lost a page is a page of a different width, so the two have to
move together or the chapter is measured against a page nobody is drawing.

## The same words twice, a few inches apart

A spread drew the book's title above each of its two pages, because the running head was part of a page
and a spread has two of them. On screen it read as a fault rather than as a running head.

The split is by what the thing names. A page number names its page, so a spread shows two, one under
each. The title names the book, so it belongs to the sheet and is set once over the binding.
`Model.showsTitle(onSheet:)` also keeps it off a sheet carrying the book's own title page, which says
what the book is called already, and off one carrying no text.

What is not touched is the band each page reserves for a head. The text still starts where it started,
so moving the title changed nothing that is measured and no book was re-paginated for it.

## Reading the device's band off the window

A folding screen can keep the system's own band down one side while the window stands upright, and the
reader's controls have always sat across the top. There is no device check that answers this and no
simulator to try, so `DeviceBand` reads it off the safe-area insets instead: one side's inset clearly
beating the other's, on a window that stands upright.

The upright condition is what makes the rule safe rather than clever. A band running along one physical
edge is at the head or the foot of a window that is turned on its side, and the head is where the
controls already are. So no phone and no tablet in any orientation can meet the test, and nothing that
ships today changes. What the condition costs is a folding screen held sideways with its band still
down an edge, which is a guess either way until there is one to hold.

Twelve points is the margin one side has to beat the other by. Below that it is the rounding of a
corner rather than a band.

`GlassRow` grew an axis for it. A column of glyph buttons is still one pane of glass, and putting the
same buttons in a `VStack` at the call site would have drawn a second capsule for the reader to notice.

## What the simulator would not do

Neither the iPhone nor the iPad simulator can be turned on its side from outside: this Xcode ships no
Simulator app, and `simctl` has no rotate. A throwaway UI test setting `XCUIDevice.shared.orientation`
worked on the iPad, and the orientation survived the test process exiting, which is what made the
landscape screenshots possible. The same test on the phone never took: every frame captured through the
run came back portrait.

So the phone's landscape page is covered by `PageSpreadTests` and by nothing on screen. A screenshot
session cannot be trusted to cover an orientation until that is sorted out.

## A phone on its side, measured on the phone

The spread's shape test started at 1.2, a page having to stand that much taller than wide before two of
them counted as a book. It was chosen against device figures rather than against a device, and on a
large phone turned on its side it was wrong: 956 by 440 halves to pages 407 wide against 440 of depth,
which is page-shaped enough to read as an opening and was being turned down.

The rule is now that a page is never wider than it is tall, and the large phone on its side opens as a
spread. A window flatter than that still keeps one page, which is what the test is for.

## A band the device did not ask for

The same screenshot showed the head and the foot of the page out of step. Turned that way the device
reports nothing at the top and the home indicator's room at the foot, so the running head sat four
points off the glass while the page number stood a good deal clear of it.

The page now keeps a band of its own at either end wherever the device asks for less, so the two ends
match. Portrait phones and tablets already report more than that at both ends and are untouched, which
is what keeps their pagination out of it: the band is part of the page size, and the page size keys
every cached measurement there is.

The controls were reading the window's own insets while the running heads read the page's, so the
moment those two differed a button no longer sat on the line its head was set on. Both read the page's
band now.

## One screenshot is not a rule

The landscape screenshot showed the text column centred on the screen, which only happens when the
leading and trailing insets match, and it was tempting to write down that every phone turned that way
reports the same inset at either edge. That is one device in one orientation, not a rule, and a sensor housing
set off-centre or a folding screen could report otherwise.

Nothing depends on it, which is the point worth keeping. `DeviceBand` turns down a side band on any
window that is not upright, so a phone on its side keeps its controls at the top whatever its insets
say.

## A plate that would not fit, and the page it left half empty

A page reported off the device ended after a handful of lines with most of it blank, and the plate that
should have followed stood alone on the next page. The cause is that `ColumnComposer.line(of:)` sizes a
picture against the whole page depth and then freezes it as a line of fixed height. A plate at two
thirds of a page can therefore follow no text at all: every break that would put it after a line
overflows, so it takes a page of its own and the page before it ends wherever the text ran out.

There were two ways out and only one of them's open. Floating the text around the plate, the way a
figure floats in print, would put the words after it before it, and a page here is one contiguous range
of the chapter. Bookmarks, selection, note anchors and reading positions all count on that. Breaking it
would mean pages holding several ranges and every one of those rewritten.

So the plate gives up depth instead, and where that decision goes is the part that matters. How much a
plate gives up depends on which break falls, and the break is what the search is choosing, so settling
it afterwards would be guessing at the answer the search had already rejected. `fitting(_:endingAt:in:)`
sits inside the search: a candidate page ending on a plate that can shrink far enough is costed as full
rather than turned away, so the run of breaks that closes the gap wins on the same arithmetic as every
other run. `cut` then works out what each page's plate actually gave, and the slack is measured against
that rather than the natural depth, or the leading would go negative on a page that is already exactly
full.

A third of its own depth is the floor. The reported case needed about fifteen per cent, and past a third
a plate reads as a different picture rather than as the same one set smaller.

Two things are deliberately left alone. A plate standing by itself keeps its size, since it's got the whole page
already, and text gives up nothing, or a page would set its own lines tighter than the column that
composed them.

The trap in the change is that four separate walks step through a page's lines: the drawing, a tap
looking for a note, a tap looking for a link, and the selection. They each had the line's height written
out in full, and one left behind would've disagreed with the drawing by a plate's worth and put every
tap below it on the wrong line, so all four read `depth(of:on:)` now.

`rulesVersion` goes to 25, which measures every book again once. That's what the pinned fingerprint
test is for: it fails on any change to the string, so a bump has to be written down rather than
noticed later as a slow first page.

## The door that asked for an account nobody had

The app opened on the sign-in screen, which is the wrong question to ask a reader whose books are their
own. It opens on the library now, whatever the session holds, and signing in is offered from the profile.

One guard was all the shelf needed. `fetchLibrary()` asked the service for a library without first
asking whether anyone had signed in, so a signed-out launch threw the reauthentication error and put
"You need to sign in to do that" over an otherwise working shelf. It reads the store and stops.

The keychain outlives a fresh install, so a test cannot reach the signed-out state by wiping the app.
`-at-ui-test-signed-out` clears it, which is what lets four tests hold the door open: the library rather
than the sign-in screen, no error over the shelf, the way to add books, and the way in from the profile.

## A book cut into a hundred and seventy chapters by its own scene breaks

Three pages reported off the device showed a row of stars standing several paragraphs later than it
belonged. The first guess was the page breaker, and it wasn't right until the file settled it.

The reports carry the chapter's markup, but that markup is already the parser's output, so it can only
say what the app made of the book and never what the book itself said. What it did say was that the three
pages straddle a chapter boundary: the last page of one chapter and the first two of the next. That
much reframed the empty half page, which is a chapter's ragged bottom and isn't a fault at all.

The file itself is what answered the rest. The break the reader expected is written
`<subtitle>⁂</subtitle>`, U+2042, the asterism, a character that means a scene break and nothing else.
The book uses it a hundred and thirty-four times and never uses anything else. `FB2Parser` read every
`<subtitle>` as a heading, and a heading cuts the book, so a file of thirty-seven chapters came out as
something over a hundred and seventy, most of them unnamed, with the mark itself swallowed by the cut
it caused. The stars the reader could actually see came from a blank line further down, sitting exactly
where that blank line was.

So a subtitle carrying nothing but the marks of a break is a break, and one carrying words is still a
heading. `BookHTML.isSceneBreak(_:)` already knew a row of asterisks, since it centres one wherever a
book leaves it justified; it now knows the asterism too and is the only test either way, so nothing can
read a block as a break by one rule and as words by another.

This overturned a deliberate decision. `cutsAtHeadingsThatNameNothing` asserted that a heading of marks
divides the book, and said so in as many words. A book whose only structure is a row of stars between
long stretches does lose its navigation by this change, which is the trade that was put to the reader
and taken.

## A break held over until the next paragraph

Reading the file turned up a second fault behind the first. The break wasn't written where it stood:
`<empty-line/>` set a flag, and the stars were written later, when the next paragraph arrived.

Two things cleared that flag without writing anything. A picture cleared it, so a break standing before
a plate was lost outright. The end of a section cleared it, so was a break closing a chapter. And
because the writing waited for a paragraph, where the break came out depended on what followed it
rather than on where the book put it.

It's written where it stands now, and the flag is gone. A run of blank lines is still one break, since
a break already written is not written again, and a break opening a block still says nothing and is
skipped. Nothing else moves: what a book set in order is what the reader meets, and a picture may only
ever be met at or after the place the file defines it, never before, or the reader is shown what
happens next.

## Marks of the reader's own, put on the page

Reading a subtitle as a break fixed where the break stood and broke something else on the way: it was
written out as a row of stars whatever the book had used. One file's hundred and thirty-two asterisms
all came out as `* * *`, which is a hundred and thirty-two rows of text that nobody wrote.

The marks are the file's own now. A blank line is the one case with none to keep, having only a gap
where a mark would be, so it still takes the row the service's own chapters use. Everything else keeps
what the book set.

Runs still collapse to one break, which now means asking whether a line already written is a break
rather than comparing it against a single string, since there is no single string any more.

## The version that makes a book read itself again

`BookReading.version` exists for exactly this: a kept file whose `readingVersion` is behind the build
re-reads itself, in the reader and in the daily sweep. Changing what the parser makes of a `<subtitle>`
without bumping it left every book on every device holding what the old parser made, and the only way
out was asking for the file again by hand.

It is the parser's half of what `rulesVersion` is to the layout, and a change to either wants the same
line changed with it.
