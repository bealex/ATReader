# 2026-09-09

## A number that hid two hundred and fifty-six different books

The first full Litres run reported 259 added and 258 failed, every failure reading `Litres.LitresError
error 0`. Two faults stacked. `LitresError` had no `description`, so a Swift enum reported its own case
index and threw away the status it came with. And the file listing decoded strictly, so one entry with
a field of an unexpected shape threw, and the throw cost the whole book rather than the entry.

`Lenient<Value>` now drops a field it can't read and keeps the rest, `malformed` carries what went
wrong, and the next run brought 516 of 517 across. The one that didn't is a genuine 404: the service
no longer hands that work out.

## The file list decides what's readable, not the type number

Five works were passed over for having `art_type 4`, a number nobody has established the meaning of,
and they were never asked about. Now every work in the library is asked what files it offers, and an
FB2 is what makes it readable. A work without one is reported as such, with its `art_type` recorded
next to it, which is how that number will eventually come to mean something.

The cost is one listing request per work that turns out not to be a book, at 400ms apiece. In a library
of five hundred, that's five requests.

## Books bought elsewhere arrive read

A book from Litres was bought and read before the app ever saw it, so it now lands with its progress
set. Libraries already synchronised were fixed in place by a migration at `user_version` 2, which runs
after `repairProgress()`, since running before it would have its work undone.

## Backing the library up to a folder

Books imported from a file live nowhere else. The store stays out of the device's own backup
deliberately, which means losing the device loses them, so `LibraryArchive` writes the whole thing to a
folder the reader picks: the database, the kept files, and a manifest.

The database goes over by `VACUUM INTO` rather than a file copy. It's open while the backup runs, and a
copy taken behind SQLite's back can catch it mid-write and carry a journal it no longer has. What comes
out is one tidy file with nothing alongside it.

Restoring closes the store, swaps the file and its `-wal` and `-shm` companions, and replaces the books
directory. The next question asked of the store opens it again, so a restore doesn't need a restart.

The folder is remembered as a security-scoped bookmark, since a URL a picker hands over stops working
the moment its scope is given up.

## A glyph has no baseline

The tick on a read row sat below the volume number and the title beside it. Not an offset gone wrong:
`Image` carries no baseline at all, so `RowStack`, which aligns on `.firstTextBaseline`, falls back to
the image's bottom edge. Nothing in the row could have lined it up.

`LineGlyph` sets the symbol as text, which puts it on the line and sizes it from the row's own font. It
sits beside `sitsOnTheLine()` as the glyph half of the same rule: a boxed label hangs below the line
because of its padding, and a symbol sits wherever its box lands because it has no line to sit on.

## Gaps fold the way read books do

A run of volumes the reader doesn't hold is four lines saying the same thing, exactly like a run of
books they have read, so the fold now takes either. One rule came with it: a run is one thing
throughout, so read books and gaps that meet each other fold into two lines rather than one line
claiming to be both.

## Two copies of one book

Two copies of one book sat on the shelf, one carried in by hand and one brought across from Litres. The
pairing compares titles, and those two copies state different volume numbers, which is the very signal
that says "two books, leave them alone". No comparison of names could have paired them.

What can is the text itself. Every local book records what its own text hashes to, so copies that hash
alike are one book whatever either file is called, and the copy the reader has got further into is the
one kept. That pass runs before names are looked at.

The cause was separate: the manual import path never checked the content hash, so a book already
brought across from a service made a second row when the same file was picked by hand. It checks now,
except when re-importing a book already on the shelf, where the text is on the device by definition and
the check would stop it being parsed again at all.

## What a cover says, and what a row says

A cover now carries where the book came from and whether its author is still writing it; the row below
carries where the reader stands in it. Ongoing left the pill row on that reasoning, since it is a fact
about the book rather than about the reading of it.

Likes went behind a setting, off unless asked for. A shelf is what the reader owns and where they are
in it, and a popularity count is the service talking about other people.

## A book nought at the head of every series

A series the reader assembled by hand opened with a book nought, and every volume after it sat one
below where it belonged. `store(series:workIds:)` filed each book under its place in the list it was
given, counted from zero the way `enumerated()` counts, and that place is read back as the book's
volume and drawn as one. A place and a volume are the same figure here, and a series counts from one.

It surfaced only once the shelf stopped holding two copies of a book. Filing rejects a series where two
books claim one volume, so a card with duplicates fell back to numbering by title and never showed the
stored figures at all.

Three parts to it: new series file from one, series already filed move up by one at `user_version` 3,
and a stated volume of nought is now read as no volume at all, since a figure that cannot be a place in
a series says nothing about where a book stands.

## A series stands on its own shelf

The series card was a list of rows: a cover for the book being read, a line each for the ones behind it,
and a folded run standing in for four or more of those. It is now a shelf. Every book is on it in both
of the card's two ways of standing, and what changes is how much room a book the reader is done with
takes up.

Covers for what is left to read; spines for what is behind them. A spine is a fifth of a cover's width,
so a fourteen-book series read to its end no longer pushes the next card off the screen. Tapping the
title switches between the two, each book flying from where it stood to where it is going, and a volume
the reader doesn't hold keeps its place in the run as a grey slot carrying only its number.

The fold went with the rows: `FoldedRun`, `ShelfRow`, the open runs and the eleven tests that held them
up. The spines say what the fold was for and say it without hiding anything.

## What a spine is made of

A blurred cover for colour, and everything else is shading. The corner radius turned out to be the wrong
instrument: a book block is square-cut, and what rounds a spine is the light falling across it. So the
radius is barely there, and the shape comes from a groove down each hinge, a pale head where the paper
shows, a dark foot, and a highlight down the middle.

Lightness is clamped rather than scaled. A ceiling on a dark shelf and a floor on a light one, taken per
channel with `.darken` and `.lighten`, so a cover keeps its hue and gives up only what stood past the
line. Taking a pale cover down with black alone walks the colour out of it, which is what made the first
attempt read as grey strips.

The writing is stamped rather than printed: a dark edge below each letter and a light one along its top.
Both offsets are given in the writing's own space, which the spine then turns on its side, so what is
written as sideways lands as up and down.

## Two bugs behind the same cache

`CoverImages` is an `NSCache` holding what has been decoded this run. A spine read from it but never
wrote to it, so it drew grey until something else on the shelf happened to load the same picture. Then
it drew grey again after every trip to the background, because iOS empties an `NSCache` there and the
spine held no reference of its own. Both the spine and the cover now keep what they draw.

## Covers fill the row

A fixed cover width leaves a ragged margin down the right that changes with the width of the screen. The
size wanted now decides how many covers go in a row, the count is rounded to a whole number of them, and
the width is shared out between that many. Measured once for the whole shelf, and measured before the
padding goes on: reading it afterwards hands the padding back and lays every cover out too wide.

## What was hiding the duplicates

Three separate things kept two copies of one book apart, and each hid the next.

The lookup key was the title *and* the author, so copies whose libraries spell a name differently never
met to be compared. The author is compared afterwards now, by the names the two lines share: one name in
common settles it where one side is a single name, otherwise two are needed, which keeps two same-titled
books by different authors apart.

The volume guard fired on the series aside itself, so a copy carrying `(Name-1)` and a copy without it
were read as different volumes. It now needs both titles to carry asides that actually differ.

And a hand-arranged series files each book's place in the list, which the guard read as a stated volume:
two copies given two places looked like two books, which is the pair that most needed joining.

Underneath all three, the shelf and the series screen disagreed, because the screen read the store
straight. It runs the same pairing now.

## The numbering a card threw away

A series the reader assembled kept its own order, and the card took that to mean it should ignore what
the titles say. But the numbering is the only thing that can say a volume is *absent*: positions counted
off the rows have no gaps in them, since every row has one. It is read for every series now. The order
the reader arranged is untouched; the numbers and the gaps are the titles' own.
