## A writer off the Reading shelf

A writer's own menu now offers to hide them from Reading, and to put them back. They keep their card
under Finished and All books, so the shelf still answers where a book went.

`hiddenFromReading` holds the keys, filed under the same key the card is, so a writer who takes a
co-author on some books stays one entry. Writing to it forgets the filing and `watchFiling` reads it,
which is what redraws the shelf. `filedShelves` drops the card in one line, and only under Reading.

The set lives in user defaults rather than the store. That makes it a per-device preference, outside
the backup, which is the wrong home if hiding should follow the reader to another device: the store is
where `madeSeries` lives, and where this would go.

The count beside the filter still counts a hidden writer's books, since it counts books rather than
cards and says how much is left to read.

## What extraction brought in

Five strings arrived in the catalogs untranslated during an ordinary `Scripts/app.sh` build: two
Litres errors, `Unknown author` in AuthorToday, and the `%@, %@` and `%@, %@, %@` author-line joiners
in BookRenderer. All five are older than today and none belong to the rename.

The first three took a translation. The joiners wouldn't. A package generates a Swift symbol per key,
and a key of nothing but format specifiers yields no name, so the build stops with `Unable to derive a
symbol name from this key`. An untranslated key is left out of that generation, which is why filling
these two in is what broke it, and why they had sat there harmlessly before. `shouldTranslate: false`
doesn't help, the entry still being there to be named. BookRenderer localises nothing but `Picture.`
and `Chapter \(position)`, so both keys were orphans and are gone.

That leaves a trap: `Scripts/check.sh` fails on an untranslated key and
the build fails on a translated one, so a joiner that extraction puts back into a package catalog has
to be deleted rather than filled in. The two gates disagree, and only the build is right.

CLAUDE.md says extraction happens when the project is opened or built in the Xcode GUI and never on a
command-line build. That wasn't so here. Correct the note once someone has pinned down which step
actually does it.

## An aside as deep as what it says

`Callout` was a scroll view inside a `maxHeight`, so every aside stood at its full depth however
little it carried: two lines of text in a card 192pt deep. It measures its content now and is set to
that.

What the cap should be came with it. `calloutDepth` was one number for every aside; it is an
environment value the overlay hands down, worked out from where the card actually stands: the room
from the edge its pointer sits on to the far side of what it covers, less the pointer and the margin,
less the notch or the home indicator where the view reaches past the safe area. In the catalogue's
own panel that is 142pt against the 154pt the card used to take.

The layer behind an aside is now `Design.Surface.dim`, black at twice the veil, in every mode. It was
`Color.black.opacity(0.001)`, which was there to catch a tap and nothing else.

That broke `CalloutMotionUITests`, which measured the card as everything in the field the bare page
did not have: under a scrim every pixel qualifies, so every frame measured the whole panel. The
reference is predicted instead. How far the scrim has come is read off a corner of the panel no aside
reaches, the bare page is darkened by that much, and what is left over is the card. The panel carries
an identifier of its own so the test knows where the scrim falls.

## A drag down the page closes the book

A screen pushed with `.zoom` is closed by dragging it back down, and the reader's never was. The first
guess was that `backSwipeDisabled()` had taken it, since that turned off `interactivePopGestureRecognizer`
outright. Putting that back changed nothing.

`ReaderSwipeUITests` is what found it. The bundled first book opens on the simulator without an
account, so a test can open it off the shelf, drag, and ask whether the library came back. With
`PageTurnView`'s `.gesture(drag)` taken out the book closed; with it in, never. SwiftUI's
`DragGesture` recognises in every direction and takes the touch at eight points, so the page owned
every drag before the transition could ask for one.

So the turn is `PageDragGesture` now, a `UIPanGestureRecognizer` that fails on a drag up or down the
page before it begins, which is what hands that drag on. The direction is read in `touchesMoved`
before the touches reach the pan: a flick arrives as one move long enough to begin it, and a pan that
has begun cannot fail.

Two things came with the port. The recognizer hangs on the window, as the press beside it does, so it
asks whether anything is presented over the page before it takes a touch; without that a drag inside
the settings turned the page behind it. And the flick thresholds used to be counted against
`DragGesture`'s predicted end translation, which is now projected from the recognizer's own velocity
at a tenth of a second.

Hit-testing the window was the wrong way to ask what covers the page. The page's text is drawn rather
than laid out in views, so the hit lands in a subtree beside the recognizer's own and is neither
ancestor nor descendant of it: every drag was refused. The controllers answer it instead.

That left the other half of what `backSwipeDisabled()` was there for: a drag in from the leading edge
closed the book instead of turning back a page. The zoom transition's dismissal answers a drag in any
direction, not only down, and `ReaderSwipeUITests` caught it doing so. `UIZoomTransitionOptions` has
the hook for it, so `Navigator` now hands every zoomed screen an
`interactiveDismissShouldBegin` that asks for the drag to be going down the screen faster than it is
going across.

`backSwipe(.downward)` stays for the stack's own gesture, which is what pops a reader opened from a
book's page rather than off the shelf, with no zoom to answer for it. It hangs a pan of the reader's
own on the window and answers `shouldBeRequiredToFailBy` for that recognizer, so a sideways drag holds
it off. Asked through the delegate rather than set with `require(toFail:)`, which cannot be taken back
off a recognizer the whole stack shares.

## Stars between scenes

A chapter's row of stars was set as an ordinary paragraph: the same gap above it as below, and an
indent where the book had not centred it.

`SceneBreak` reads one off the paragraph, `ChapterPagination` centres it and sets it in air, and
`ChapterLayout` counts one at the head of a page as a broken rule. The air is what carries the
meaning, and at the top of a page it falls off the top, so the page before it takes the stars and the
scene that follows opens a page of its own.

The air was first counted in the gap paragraphs take between them, four of those above and two below.
On a page reported off a device it came to nothing: that gap is four fifths of the reader's line
spacing, which was three points, so four of them is under ten. It is counted in lines of the page
now, which holds at any setting: three above the stars and five below. Rules version 15.

The source marks these, as it happens. Every one of the 133 in that book is an FB2 `<subtitle>`, which
`FB2Parser` writes out as a centred paragraph like any other. The detection stays on the characters
rather than the element: a chapter from the service arrives as HTML with no such element, and the one
`<subtitle>` in that book that is a real subtitle should not be given a scene break's air.

What counts as one: stars and the spaces between them, seven marks at the most, so a line of
asterisks is still read as text. Format characters are skipped, since by then the typesetter has been
through the text.

## Formulae keep their figures

`BookHTML.plainText` strips every tag with one regular expression, so `CH<sub>3</sub>OH` reached the
page as `CH3OH`. The book that showed it up carries six of them in one note and no `<sup>` at all,
but both are read now.

They ride the fencing the note markers already use: `<sub>` and `<sup>` become private-use characters
before the markup is flattened, which the tag stripper has no opinion about, and are read back out of
the flat text as ranges. So nothing has to parse the markup twice, and the characters the text arrived
with are exactly the characters that come out, which is what keeps every reading position where it is.

`ChapterContent` moves the ranges when binding and hyphenation put characters in, the same way it
moves a note's marker. `ChapterPagination` sets them at the marker's size, dropped by `ScriptMarker`
or lifted by `NoteMarker.rise`. A lift takes a negative offset and a drop a positive one, the page
being drawn through a flipped matrix. Typography version 7, which re-prepares what is already stored.

## Bookmarks

A `bookmark` table, keyed on the work, the chapter and where the mark begins, so a mark can be found
again and taken off. Offsets rather than pages, for the reason a reading position is one: a page set
in one face is a different page set in another.

The reader's bar marks what is on screen and clears what the page stands on, which is the same button
either way. A page carrying two chapters marks both stretches, since both are what the reader can see.

Touching ends are not an overlap. A mark stopping exactly where the next page begins belongs to the
page before it; counted the other way, every page would open showing itself already marked.

The marks stand under their chapters in the reader's contents and in the book's details, indented
rather than badged: a list of chapters with marks under them reads as one list, and a mark standing
level with a chapter reads as another chapter. How far into the chapter each one is comes from the
chapter's own length, from the layout where one has been built and from what the service said
otherwise.

## The air landed under the words

The title blocks went out and the stars still stood tight, twice, for two different reasons and
neither of them the spacing.

The first was the prepared-chapter cache. A chapter is kept against a hash of its source and
`Typography.version`, and the version had been moved for the subscripts but not again for the title
levels. So every book already on a device went on being read back through paragraphs parsed before
levels existed, carrying none, while its pages were re-measured under the new rules. The report showed
it in one line: `rulesVersion: 16` beside `typographyVersion: 7`, which cannot both be current. The
constant's own documentation said to bump it whenever the prepared text changes; it now says a new
field on `Paragraph` counts, since that is the half that got missed.

The second was the drawing. A page draws each line at `cursor + baseline` and then steps on by that
line's height. The air went into the height and the baseline stayed at the font's ascender, so the
glyphs kept their place at the top of a taller box and every bit of the air fell underneath them: a
gap after the stars and none before. The baseline clears the air now, and `TypesetLine` publishes it
so a test can say which side the air is on.

## Stars are a title because the book said so

The centred-paragraph-of-stars rule is gone. A paragraph is centred for all sorts of reasons, an
epigraph and a dedication among them, so what one holds is no longer asked: the level comes from a
heading element, and `FB2Parser` writes a file's `<subtitle>` out as one.

The cost is that a book filed before that was written carries its subtitles as centred paragraphs, and
its breaks stand in no air until its file is read again. There is no way to ask for that yet.

## The reader is presented, not pushed

The zoom transition works for a presentation as well as a push, and the push was what made it jump: a
pushed screen takes the tab bar away on the way in and gives it back on the way out, and the stack's
own bar with it, all of it re-laying out while the zoom runs. Presented, it covers them instead and
nothing underneath moves.

Two things went with it. The reader carries a `NavigationStack` of its own for its bar and a Close
button, a presented screen having no back button. And `backSwipe` is gone from it: the stack's own
gesture is behind the presentation now, so there is nothing left to hold off.

One thing broke on the way. `PageDragGesture` refuses a touch while anything is presented over the
page, which it worked out by walking the responder chain — and a reader presented over the library
found the library presenting *it*, so every drag read as covered and no page turned. It walks parents
now, which stop at the presentation's own edge.

## Adding books

`AddBooksScreen` is where a book gets onto the device: a file, the Litres shelf, or a catalogue. It
opens from a `+` on the library's bar, and the section that used to sit in Profile is gone.

`OPDS` is the eighth package, Foundation and `XMLParser` and nothing else. It asks a catalogue for its
own search address, which is either a link the feed carries or an OpenSearch description to fetch
first, puts the query into the template, and reads the Atom answer back as books with acquisition
links. What shape a link leads to is read from the media type and then from the address, because
catalogues disagree about how to spell it and some send `application/octet-stream` with the answer
only in the path.

The address is the reader's own and is kept in user defaults, since every library has a different one.

## The zoom landed somewhere the book no longer was

Presenting the reader took the bars out of the transition, and it still jumped at the end. A screen
recording said why: a frame before the end the cover is one width, and the frame after it, with the
transition's snapshot gone, the real cover underneath is narrower.

`preferredTransition = .zoom { _ in source }` was closing over the view the shelf handed across when
the book opened. UIKit asks that closure again on the way out, and a shelf is a collection view whose
cells are reused: while the book was open the shelf laid itself out again around what the reading had
changed, so the board being shrunk into was not the board that had been grown out of.

The shelf hands over a closure now, `{ [weak self] in self?.books[place.id]?.face }`, so the question
is answered at the moment it is asked, in both directions.

### And what the jump actually was

Every frame of the last second was differenced against the one before it. The screen is still from
2.03s to 2.35s, one frame changes 166,000 pixels, and it is still again afterwards: a single relayout
landing after the transition had already ended, rather than an animation finishing badly.

The frame before it shows a shelf that has not finished drawing. The cover floats above the shelf line
it belongs on, and the spine numbers in the row above are missing: 13, three blanks, 8. The frame
after shows every number drawn and the cover seated.

`.fullScreen` takes the presenting view out of the window. So the shelf was off the window for the
whole reading session, and built its cells and printed its spines again only once the reader had gone.
`.overFullScreen` leaves it there to keep up while it is covered.

### The frame the transition was given

Three explanations for the jump were wrong. The bars re-laying out, the source view being captured
rather than looked up, and the shelf doing its work off-window under `.fullScreen` were all real and
all worth fixing, and none of them was it.

What it was: the view handed to the zoom was `facePanel`, and a panel is stood by setting
`layer.position` with `anchorPoint` at zero and then turned by a 3D transform. `BookView` already
said so in a comment of its own — "a frame means nothing once a transform is on it" — and a frame is
precisely what a zoom transition reads off the view it is given. So the book grew out of a rectangle
the artwork was never in, and shrank back into the same wrong one, and the real cover appeared in its
proper place the moment the transition let go.

`zoomAnchor` is a plain view with no transform, laid at the face's own rectangle in `layoutSubviews`,
drawing nothing and taking no touches. That is what the shelf hands over now.

The frame was half of it. A zoom shrinks the screen into what its source view is *showing*, and the
anchor was showing nothing, so the book shrank into an empty rectangle and the cover appeared when the
transition let go.

The anchor carries the whole cover now, not only its artwork: `CoverView.picture()` renders the layer,
so the line read, the ribbon and the crease come with it. Taken when the transition asks rather than
kept up to date, since nothing else ever wants it and `place(_:)` runs on every frame of a turn.

## What a real catalogue taught the OPDS client

Tried against a live server, the search found nothing, and both reasons were conformance bugs of ours
rather than anything about that server.

**It preferred the wrong address.** A feed may advertise a search two ways: an inline `rel="search"`
link carrying `{searchTerms}`, and an OpenSearch description to fetch. Ours took the inline one, and
the server answered that path with a 502 while the description pointed at a path that works. The
description is preferred now. It is the standard's own mechanism, it can carry several templates and
say what each answers in, and a catalogue that publishes one has said more about itself than a
convention link does. The inline link stays as the fallback for a catalogue with no description.

**It left emptied parameters behind.** A template carries more than the query, and the standard says
an optional parameter nobody fills is left out. Replacing the placeholder with nothing is not leaving
it out: `pageNumber={startPage?}` came out as `pageNumber=`, and a catalogue reading that as a page
number answers nothing. Whole pairs go now.

Paging follows the feed's own `rel="next"` rather than counting pages into the address, since that is
the only thing that knows when an answer has run out. The first request does fill `{startPage}`, from
the description's `pageOffset`: the standard's default first page is one and that server counts from
nought, so it has to be asked rather than assumed.

### One definition of a line

Cutting the chapter heading's air back to two lines failed its own test, and the test was right. The
air was laid down in `ChapterPagination` counting a line as `font.lineHeight + lineSpacing`, and cut
back in `ChapterLayout` counting it as `fontSize + lineSpacing`: about 29.7 points against 26 at the
settings the tests use, so the trim took more than it had been asked for.

`ChapterTextStyle.pageLine` is the one definition now, and both sides ask it. The page rule that keeps
a title standing in three lines or more off the head of a page asks it too, since that threshold is
counted in the same lines. What is left counting `fontSize + lineSpacing` is `referenceLineHeight`,
which prices how short a page came out and is about filling a page rather than about air.

## Where the test time actually goes

`app.sh` grew two ways to overlap runs, because it had neither: `--parallel` passes
`-parallel-testing-enabled` through, and `DD` is overridable so two invocations can have derived data
of their own. The path was hardcoded, which is why a build under a running suite failed it in ways
that read as real test failures.

Then the numbers were taken, which changed the answer. A build from empty derived data is 26 seconds.
The 227 unit tests run in 15. The four reader UI tests take 75, nearly all of it launching the app
four times and waiting for the library. Compiling is not slow and never was: a ten-minute run had
done 31 compiles where a hundred-second run had done 71.

What the ten minutes was, was `--parallel` cloning simulators. It is also not safe here: the tests
that import a book share that state and fail under it, cloned simulators isolating containers rather
than the assumption that nobody else is importing. So the flag stays, documented as not for these
suites, and the thing to keep warm is the simulator.

Twenty seconds came off the reader's UI tests by taking out the fixed sleeps. Waiting five seconds
for a page to be drawn is five seconds every fast run pays too; waiting for the page to publish its
words is over as soon as it is true. 95 seconds to 75.

## Browsing a catalogue, not only searching it

The OPDS screen asks for one of three things now: a book, an author or a series. A book is searched
for by any part of its name through the catalogue's own OpenSearch address. An author or a series is
looked up by what the name begins with, since that is how a catalogue cuts its indexes.

Nothing is searched for until the button is pressed. A catalogue is somebody else's server and often a
slow one, and searching for every letter typed is searching for every letter typed.

The sections are the catalogue's own. A root feed lists what it keeps, one entry per section, and the
one wanted is found by what its address says rather than by a path written down here: that server
offers `По авторам` at `/opds/authorsindex` and `По сериям` at `/opds/sequencesindex`, and another
server will offer its own. Narrowing a section by putting the prefix on the end of its address is a
convention rather than anything the standard says, so it is tried and quietly given up on: what is
left is the section itself, browsed down by hand.

Which is the same screen again. A catalogue is a tree, every level of it looks alike, and an entry
carrying a link to another feed and nothing to acquire is a branch. A feed holding exactly one branch
is followed at once, up to eight deep, since an index narrowed to a single answer is asking to be
stepped through rather than tapped through.

A book whose only shapes this reader cannot open is still listed, greyed and marked, and cannot be
tapped. Dropping it would read as a catalogue missing books rather than as a book in the wrong
wrapping.

## Marking a book read threw away where the reader was

A book marked read from its first page reopened at its last. Marking read set the progress to one and
then wrote a reading position at the end of the final chapter, because the chapter marks on a book's
page were drawn from the position and that was how they were made to fill.

Two things went wrong with that. The place the reader had actually reached was gone, which is the
whole complaint. And `store(position:)` recomputes progress from the position it was given, so the
one set a moment earlier was immediately overwritten by whatever the end of the last chapter worked
out to: a row reading finished beside ninety-six per cent.

The position write is gone. `state(of:)` answers read for every chapter of a book that has been read
through, which is what the write was for, and the two facts stay apart: what the reader did with a
book, and where they are in it.

## Cutting a book that never says where its chapters are

Some files mark their chapters with sections, some with headings inside one section, and some mark
nothing at all. The last kind arrives as a single chapter however long it runs, which is worth
avoiding: a chapter holding a whole book is composed from scratch every time the book is opened.

So a file that yields exactly one section is cut at the headings inside it. Only that case, since a
file that already says where its chapters are has said so. A heading carrying no letters or digits is
a break between scenes and no place to start a chapter, which is what most of these headings turn out
to be: the book that prompted this has ninety-eight of them and every one is three stars, so it stays
one chapter and the cutting leaves it alone.

## Keeping the broken lines

Opening a book re-broke every chapter it touched, which is the expensive half of laying one out and
the reason a long chapter took seconds to appear. Where a chapter's pages fall was already kept, in
`chapter_placement`, but that pass has to compose each chapter to find out, so a book measured for the
first time paid for all of it and a chapter being read paid again.

The lines are now kept too, in `chapter_column`, as zlib-compressed JSON. They depend on the text and
the setting and not on where the chapter starts on its page, so cutting them into pages stays where it
was and runs afresh every time, costing nothing worth measuring.

`ColumnComposer.Line` and its `Setting` are coded by hand rather than synthesised. An `NSRange` is
written as two integers, and the two fields holding a decoded image are left out: a picture belongs in
the file it was unpacked to, not in the database a second time. A chapter carrying one is composed the
long way, which `isKeepable` decides.

The file name is a hash of the setting's fingerprint and the chapter's own text, so a corrected file
or a change of type misses rather than matches. The fingerprint already carries
`ChapterLayout.rulesVersion`, which is what keeps a change to the breaking rules from leaving books
drawing yesterday's lines.

The failure this can produce is text set subtly wrong rather than a crash, so `ColumnCacheTests`
compares a restored chapter to a freshly composed one line for line: text, width, height, baseline,
gap multiple, and every flag. It then shortens the kept column and rebuilds, because two runs agreeing
proves nothing on its own if the second run quietly composed again.

## Where opening a book actually went

A book opened with two waits: a spinner for the chapter, then a bar for the measuring, and then ten
seconds in which the page was on screen and would not turn. `OpeningCostTests` opens a book from a
backup the way the reader does, at the setting off a device report, and watches the main actor with a
heartbeat: a stretch the beat cannot get in on is a stretch in which no gesture is recognised.

A thirty-six-chapter book, cold, in Debug on a simulator:

| | before | after |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the whole pass | 23.3 s | 5.9 s |
| main actor held past 100 ms | 52 times, 17.8 s in all | never |
| the longest single hold | 473 ms | 13 ms |

Timing the phases inside the layout put nearly all of it in one place. Per chapter: typesetting 4 ms,
reading a kept column 3 ms, composing 60 ms, and **cutting the column into pages 344 ms**, on the main
actor every time. The reader was told the composing was the expensive half; it is a twentieth of it.

Two things were wrong with the cut. It ran on the main actor, where the reader's gestures live. And it
read a `ColumnComposer.Line` twenty-odd times per line, each read copying a struct holding a picture
and two strings, so the search paid reference counts for questions like whether the line ends in a
hyphen.

`PageCutter` now holds a flattened copy of the column, one `Slug` per line carrying the depth and the
five flags a rule asks about, and runs the whole cut in a detached task. Both together: the pass is
four times quicker and the main actor is never held. The 181-chapter book, the longest in that
library, measures cold in 9 seconds and holds the main actor for 16 ms at worst.

The two waits are one card now. Fetching a chapter and measuring the book behind it are one wait to
whoever is waiting, so `ProgressBar` slides until the measuring can say how far along it is and fills
from then on.

What is left is honest work: preparing a chapter's text, which is parsing and hyphenation and already
runs off the main actor, and the measuring itself. Reopening a book measured at the same setting costs
nothing, because the placements are read back: 37 ms for the whole of it.

A quarter of the cuts are still made twice. A chapter that runs on from the page before it is laid out
at that offset and asked how much of itself landed, and the answer is only reachable by cutting it, so
a chapter that turns out to need a page of its own is cut again from zero. The search's own table is
the same either way except for its first row, which is the way out of that when it is worth taking.

## The rest of opening a book

Three more, against the same thirty-six-chapter book measured the same way. The harness changed with
them: it used to prepare each chapter's text itself and hand it over, which is not how the reader gives
it to the pass, and with preparing and measuring overlapping the two can no longer be told apart. The
total is the honest number either way.

| | measuring | preparing and measuring together |
| --- | --- | --- |
| the cut off the main actor | 3.0 s | 5.9 s |
| the search's table reused | 2.4 s | 5.1 s |
| the next chapter read early | — | 3.0 s |

**The search's table.** A chapter that runs on from the page before it is laid out at that offset and
asked how much of itself landed, and a chapter that could not bring three lines was cut again from
zero: fifty-two cuts for thirty-six chapters. Only the first page's depth turns on where a chapter
starts, so every row of the search but its first is the same either way. `PageCutter.Breaks` is that
table and `ChapterLayout.recut(startOffset:)` cuts from it, so the chapter is cut twice and searched
once. `RunOnTests` compares a recut chapter to one cut from the start line for line and page for page,
because getting this wrong moves page breaks rather than failing outright.

**Reading ahead.** Preparing a chapter is parsing and hyphenating it, and depends on nothing else in
the book, so the pass starts the next chapter's while it lays this one out. It waits for one of the two
instead of both. The read-ahead belongs to the pass rather than to one call of it, since the pass
behind the reader measures a chapter per call and would otherwise throw it away every time. It is only
started behind a chapter that had to be laid out, which is what keeps a reopened book from touching its
text: that reopen is 3 ms for thirty-six chapters and 15 ms for a hundred and eighty-one.

**Who keeps a column.** Encoding and compressing one ran on the main actor, about ten milliseconds a
chapter, which is what was left of the holds the heartbeat could see. It runs in a detached task now,
and the worst hold over the longest book in the library went from 18 ms to 7 ms. The measuring pass
also stopped keeping columns at all: it throws every layout away, so it was writing one per chapter to
have one or two read back. It gets a `ColumnsToRead`, and the chapters worth keeping are kept by the
reader, which is where they are asked for.

## A heading is two things

A chapter heading is set as the chapter's place, small and letter-spaced above, and the chapter's name,
large under it. Books that write both into one title got neither: "Глава 17 Край солёных озёр" came
out as one long bold line, because a title that numbers itself had its generated number dropped and
was set whole.

`HeadingNumbering` parts it. A word the heading opens with that names a part of a book, the numeral
after it, and the name that follows. The numeral is a figure, a roman numeral, or a numeral in words,
and the word case is where the guessing lives: Russian numbers a chapter with an ordinal, so a
cardinal at the front is the name counting something and "Глава Три товарища" stays whole, while
English says "Chapter One" and is read either way. A figure ends the numbering where it stands, so
"Глава 5 Три товарища" parts after the five. Without a numeral there has to be a separator, which is
what keeps "Часть тела" off the page as a part of anything.

Where nothing can be parted the heading stands as it is, which is what "Пролог" and "Глава 17" want
anyway. Where a heading carries its own number, that number is used rather than the chapter's count:
a book with a prologue in it disagrees with the count and is right.

The air above a first-level title went from twelve lines to eight. Twelve was chosen so chapters
mostly start a new page; eight still does that and leaves the heading less stranded at the top of the
page it opens.

The reader's running head showed the book's title as the service spells it, series and all: "Кроу
неуловимый (Альфа-14)". It shows what the shelf calls the book now, which is `SeriesNumbering.title`
with the repeated words taken off. The title page too, which names the series on a line of its own and
was saying it twice.

## Two smaller things on the shelf and the page

The reader's running head was moved to the leading edge and put straight back in the middle, which is
where it reads best over a centred page. It stands as it stood.

**Hide series**, in the library's filter menu, is the first rule that picks through a card rather than
keeping or hiding it whole. A library that follows long series stands mostly on volumes nobody has
opened, and finding where the reading is means reading past them. With it on, a series keeps the books
that have been opened and drops the rest, which leaves a series nobody has opened with nothing to show
and takes it off the shelf.

Two things it does not touch. A book that arrived in the last day stays, opened or not: that is
`Book.isJustTakenDown`, and a day is as long as anything else stands out on this shelf for. And a run
of one book is left alone, since a single book is not a series and hiding it would hide a book.
A search stands the hiding aside, the same way it already stands the filter aside.

It shipped not working, and the reason is worth keeping. `watchFiling()` reads every property the
filing is worked out from, so that whatever shows the shelf is subscribed to all of them: the caches
themselves are `@ObservationIgnored`, since a filing of a couple of thousand books is worked out once
and read on every redraw. A new switch that the filing depends on and `watchFiling()` does not name
clears the cache and tells nobody, so the shelf goes on drawing what it drew. Anything that changes
what the filing produces belongs in that function.
