# 2026-09-07

## A series read through was still twenty lines

A book behind the reader was already one line inside a series card, a tick and its title. That's short
enough for a trilogy and not for a long series: someone twenty books in scrolled twenty ticks to reach
the one they were reading, and every one of those lines said the same thing.

A run of more than three of them now folds into a single line carrying the volumes it covers and nothing
else: `1`, an ellipsis, `17`. A count of the books read was there first and came back out. The numbers
already say how many, and a row of two badges reads in one glance where a row with a sentence in it asks
to be read. Three or fewer stays as it was, since three lines are quicker to read past than a line
asking to be tapped.

## Why the run has to be sequential

Folding every read book in a card would have been simpler and would have lied about the shape of the
series. A gap the reader hasn't filled, or a book they're partway through, is exactly what they're
looking for on that card. So the fold walks the rows the card already produces and breaks on anything
that isn't a finished book. That covers a missing volume for free: a gap row is not a book, so it ends
the run it interrupts.

## An opened run lasts until the next scroll

The opened runs live on the model as a set of ids, and the first drag on the shelf empties it. Nothing
persists, so a reader who opens three runs on the way down a long library doesn't find them still open
tomorrow, and there's no state to store or migrate. `onScrollPhaseChange` fires on `.interacting`
rather than `.tracking`, so the touch that opens a run can't close it on the way in.

Picking books out leaves every row showing. A fold would hide the books the reader is reaching for, and
the point of that mode is reaching them.

## Everything in a row sits on one line

The first draft of the folded line had the badge and the words beside it drifting apart, and so did the
finished-book lines it sits among, and the book page's title. Every one of them was already written as
`HStack(alignment: .firstTextBaseline)`, so the alignment wasn't missing. What was missing is that a
badge is text inside a padded ground: a baseline drawn through its text hangs the ground three points
below the line, and next to larger words the box reads low.

`sitsOnTheLine()` takes that padding back out of the baseline the badge offers its row, which puts the
box on the line instead of under it. `Pill` wears it too, since a pill is the same shape with a capsule
around it.

The alignment itself is now a component rather than a habit. `RowStack` is an `HStack` that aligns on
the first text baseline, and it's what anything side by side is written with; a stack that wants some
other alignment stays an `HStack` and says so by being one. Named `RowStack` rather than `Row` because
a row already means a row of a list here, and `BookRow`, `SeriesRow` and `ShelfRow` were all taken.

The catalogue carries a **Baseline** specimen: a badge against each of the text roles, the shelf's
folded run, and one row holding a glyph, a badge, words and a pill at once. Alignment is the kind of
thing that only fails against a neighbour, so the specimen is a set of neighbours.
