# 2026-09-03

## The column breaks its own lines

TextKit no longer sets the column. `ColumnComposer` chooses every break in a paragraph and settles how
each line is filled as one decision, and CoreText is left with measuring and drawing.

## Why it was worth doing

TextKit breaks greedily: it takes all it can for each line in turn, then justifies whatever it took.
The line before a long word is left holding too little and has to open wide, and the text that would
have filled it is above, in lines already settled. Everything that used to sit between `collectLines`
and `composePages` existed to undo that: `rebreakCrowdedLines` re-costing a window of breaks,
`loosenCrowdedLines` re-setting the lines it couldn't fix, and two whole re-layouts of the chapter in
`freedText` and `tightenedText`. All of it is gone.

The pieces that made a manual breaker cheap were already written. `Typography.hyphenated` had been
marking break points from the system dictionary for a while, so the breaker inherited them; the
paragraph-wide cost function already existed for the rebreak window; and loosened lines were already
drawn run by run.

## The measurements

A generated chapter of 244 KB, 19pt serif at 24pt margins:

| | lines | pages | time |
|---|---|---|---|
| TextKit | 4002 | 160 | 5983 ms |
| `ColumnComposer` | 3602 | 142 | 702 ms |

Eight generated chapters at the same setting, a measure of about 26 lines: all 25 pages that don't end
a chapter carry 24, 25 or 26 lines. The eight short pages are the eight chapter endings.

`ChapterLayout` went from 1596 lines to 432.

## How the slack is shared

The gaps and the letters reach their ceilings at the same moment, so the ratio between them falls out
of the line rather than being fixed in advance. A first attempt set the gap ceiling at its absolute
limit of 6.2 spaces, which left the letters with about a fifth of the capacity and looked much like the
old setting. Splitting it in two fixed that: gaps and letters share a comfortable range, and past that
the gaps go on alone to the absolute limit while the tracking stops.

Costing that second range heavily is what straightened the column. A line whose gaps had to go
on alone costs several times what a broken word does, so the breaker hyphenates or moves a word instead
of leaving a hole.

## Three traps

- **CoreText reads a colour key of its own.** `NSAttributedString.Key.foregroundColor` is what TextKit
  reads; CoreText wants `kCTForegroundColorAttributeName` and a `CGColor`, and falls back to black
  without it. Every page drew black in the dark theme until the ruler bridged the two.
- **Kern after a line's last character is advance belonging to no glyph.** It counts towards the line's
  width without moving any ink, which is what pushed a tracked line past its measure. The kern is
  applied to every character but the last.
- **Soft hyphens and joiners are stripped before measuring and again before drawing.** Whether CoreText
  gives a default-ignorable character zero width is not worth depending on, and taking them out of both
  paths means the two can't disagree by construction.

## What this closed

- Letters take their share of the slack alongside the gaps rather than only what the gaps leave.
- Nothing caps consecutive hyphenated lines: a second broken word directly under the first now costs.
- A paragraph's last line may be one short word: a stub last line now costs, so the line above gives a
  word down.
- `theEdgeHolds` counting short lines instead of asking why: gone with the test it was in.

## The render harness

`PageRenderTests` writes pages to PNG when `TEST_RUNNER_AT_RENDER_DIR` names a directory, with a rule
down each margin. Running it before and after a change and looking at the two directories answered in a
glance what the line-by-line report took a lot longer to.

One note on running it: a test that records an issue makes `xcodebuild` spend 600 seconds collecting
diagnostics from the simulator afterwards. The tests themselves finish in about two seconds, so a
ten-minute wait means a failure, not a slow layout.

## Pictures in the column

The reader draws illustrations. `FB2Parser` keeps the binaries the text points at, `BookImport` writes
them out beside the book, and `ColumnComposer` sets each one as a line of the column with a depth of
its own. Read `Documentation/Reader.md` on pictures for the rules.

### Why a picture is a line

The alternative was a second pass that placed pictures after the text was broken, and it fails the
moment a plate is taller than what is left of a page: something has to move, and by then the page
breaks are settled. Making it a line puts it in front of the same breaker that already knows how to
cost a page that came out short, so a plate too deep for the space left simply starts the next page.

It also settled the reading position for nothing. A picture is one character in the laid-out text, so
an offset counts it the way it counts a paragraph and survives a change of font.

### What the pixels had to answer

The sample book carries 29 plates, and every one of them is a single ink drawn entirely in alpha: a
40-entry palette of pure black with the tRNS chunk running 0 to 255. Composited on white they are line
art; composited on a night page they were a white rectangle in the middle of the text.

So the question the pixels have to answer isn't "is this greyscale" but "what is this drawn on". Once
the ground is known, coverage is distance from it scaled by alpha, and that's one formula for every
case rather than a branch per file format: it turns white-on-black art the right way up as readily as
it reads a scan.

Keeping that coverage as a stencil rather than a tinted bitmap is what made the reader's setting cheap
to add: it's one boolean on a path the ink pictures already take.

### Two traps

- **`CGContext.clip(to:mask:)` with a grey mask paints where the mask is white**, and the opposite
  convention holds for a `CGImage` image mask. Getting it backwards draws a perfect negative and
  nothing else complains, so `drawsInkInThePagesColour` reads the pixels back.
- **CoreGraphics counts upwards and both UIKit and SwiftUI count down.** A picture drawn without
  turning the context over comes out on its head, and art is still art upside down.
  `drawsAPictureTheRightWayUp` pins it in both contexts, since the title page's cover goes through a
  SwiftUI `Canvas` rather than the page's own UIKit context. Removing the flip fails four expectations,
  which is how the test was checked.

### Measuring it

`IllustratedBookTests` takes an FB2 named in `AT_TEST_BOOK` all the way through: parse, import into a
store of its own, lay out, and draw the pages carrying plates into `AT_RENDER_DIR`, one per theme and
one per setting. No book belongs in this repository, so it does nothing without the variable.

On the sample book the paper and night renders came back with an ink map correlating at 1.0000: the
same drawing, each page in its own two colours.

## Zipped books, and keeping the file

Two things followed from pictures, both because an imported book's text is whatever the parser made of
its file and nothing kept the file.

### Reading an archive

FB2 is handed out zipped more often than not, and the app took only plain files. There is no public zip
reader on the platform, so `ZipArchive` reads the part of the format a book needs: the central
directory, and members that are stored or deflated.

Two details did the work. The sizes come from the central directory rather than the local headers,
because a zip written as a stream leaves them zero there and fills them in afterwards. And a zip
member's payload is raw DEFLATE, which is exactly what `NSData.decompressed(using: .zlib)` reads, so no
inflater had to be written.

It is checked against archives the system makes rather than ones written to match it:
`NSFileCoordinator` with `.forUploading` zips a folder, and `BookFileTests` reads what it produced. The
sample book zips to 4.7 MB from 7.1 MB and comes back through the whole path with its 29 plates.

### Keeping the file

An imported book was a dead end: its chapter bodies were frozen at import, the source file was gone,
and a parser that learned something new could not be applied to anything. That is exactly what pictures
hit, and every book already on a device had to be found and picked again by hand.

So the file is kept, compressed, and the book screen's menu re-reads it in place. Compression is worth
about a third and no more, because the pictures inside are already compressed and base64 is most of
what remains.

The trap is in the deleting. `removeBook` drops the `reading_position` row along with the chapters, so
delete-then-import loses the reader's place, while importing over the top keeps it: the same file
fingerprints the same way and lands on the same row. The menu re-imports; it never deletes.

A security-scoped bookmark was the alternative, at a few hundred bytes against several megabytes. It
was turned down for going stale whenever a file is moved, renamed or handed over into a temp inbox,
which would have left the same manual hunt in place for some books and not others.

## Pictures keep their own light and dark

The ground-detection rule above was replaced the same day. It mapped a picture's ink onto the page's
foreground and its ground onto the background, which inverted every drawing on a dark theme: line art
came out as white strokes on black, a negative of itself.

The rule now is that a picture keeps its polarity. Black lands on the deeper of the page's two colours
and white on the paler one, whichever of the foreground and background each happens to be, and what was
never drawn on counts as light. So a plate reads as ink on paper under every theme, in that theme's own
two colours.

That deleted the ground detection entirely, along with the border sampling and the polarity branch it
fed. What is kept is `(1 - luminance) × alpha`, and drawing is two fills: the paler colour laid down
whole, then the deeper one painted through the shading. A grey comes out a mixture rather than one or
the other, which is what the old single-colour fill through a coverage mask could not do.

It has a consequence worth seeing before judging it: on a dark theme a plate is now a pale block in the
middle of the page, because its paper is the paler of the two colours. On Night that is the theme's own
grey rather than white, and on Green the plate is a green field with near-black ink.

## Pictures are centred in their space

A page's leftover room used to spread between its lines up to 3pt a gap and then stop, leaving the rest
as a hole at the foot. Where a plate takes two thirds of the page there is far more left over than the
gaps can take, so what the leading cannot absorb now goes around the pictures instead.

Each picture is centred in everything the page gave it, its own line's trailing spacing included. That
last part is what the first attempt got wrong: prepending the padding left the line's 12.6pt of
paragraph spacing below the picture, so it sat 12.6pt above centre and `centresAPictureThatIsTheWholePage`
caught it.

A page that ends a chapter still keeps its ragged bottom, unless it holds nothing but a picture.

### A trap in reading pixels back

`UIGraphicsImageRendererFormat.preferred()` gives a wide-gamut bitmap of sixteen bits a component, so
the first byte of a white pixel is zero. A test that scanned it for dark pixels found every row of the
page covered in ink. Reading a rendered page back means drawing it into a bitmap of a known shape
first, which is what `inkBand` does.

## The shelf reads as a set

Three changes to the library, all of them about a series being one thing rather than a run of books.

**The filter runs over cards.** It used to pick through books and then group whatever survived, so a
series the reader was partway through showed only its unread half and the book they had just finished
vanished from under the one they were on. Now a card is kept or hidden whole: anything left in a series
keeps all of it, and only a series read to its last book is finished. The two filters are a division of
the shelf, which `everyCardIsUnderOneFilterOrTheOther` pins.

**A finished book in a series is one line.** A tick and its title. Given a cover and three lines each,
the books behind the reader push the one being read off the screen. Being caught up on a book still
being written doesn't collapse it, and neither does one with new chapters: that badge is the point.

**The cover opens the book; the rest opens its page.** Two tap gestures rather than a button and a
link. The cover sits inside the row's own target, and a gesture on it is the inner one, which is the
one SwiftUI recognises first; two nested buttons can both fire. VoiceOver keeps one element per row
with a `Read` action on it.

`LibraryFilterTests` is the first test the library model has had. It exercises the filter rule alone,
which needs no model instance and no store.

## A plate on a chapter's last page

Centring a picture skipped any page that ended a chapter unless the page held nothing else, which is
the wrong test. A part title is a heading and a plate and nothing after it, so the plate hung from the
heading with a quarter of the page empty beneath it.

The rule the carve-out was reaching for is about where the spare room stands. A page that ends a
chapter keeps its ragged bottom, so its leftover sits after the last line rather than being spread
through the page. Only a picture that *is* that last line has any of that room beneath it, and only
then is there anything to centre it in. A picture with the chapter's last words below it is already
where it belongs.

So the leading and the padding are worked out separately now: the leading is what it always was, zero
on a last page, and the pictures take whatever the leading could not absorb. On a last page the count
of pictures sharing it is the trailing run rather than all of them.

Measured on a book of part titles, the space above a plate and the space below it come within about
10pt of each other. The residual is the blank line under a heading, which belongs to the heading and
stands above the picture.
