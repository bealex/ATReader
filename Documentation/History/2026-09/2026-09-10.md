# 2026-09-10

## The whole library's spines, printed before it is scrolled

A spine is a blurred, resaturated copy of its own cover, and it was printed the moment a card scrolled
into view: a CoreImage blur, an area average and a render, per book, on the main thread, in the middle
of a frame. A card of twenty books paid twenty of those at once.

`SpinePress` prints them all ahead of the reader instead. It walks the cards in the order they stand,
asks the cover cache for what the device already has, and hands each order to an actor holding a
CoreImage context of its own, at utility priority. What comes back is filed on the main actor. Asked
for the same set again it does nothing, so a shelf redrawn because one cover landed doesn't start over.

The drawing had been reading three things off wherever it happened to run, and each is handed to it
now. `SpinePrint.draw` takes the screen's density and gives the image renderer a format carrying it,
rather than letting the renderer pick up the main screen's. It takes the CoreImage context too, since a
context belongs to whoever draws with it and `CIContext` can't cross an isolation boundary. And the one
dynamic colour a spine uses, the ground under a book whose cover hasn't arrived, settles against its own
scheme rather than against `UITraitCollection.current`, which off the main thread is nobody's.

`SpinePressTests` compares the pixels of a spine printed on the main actor with the same spine printed
off it. Nothing else catches a colour, a scale or a filter quietly landing somewhere different.

## A cache with no bound is a cache that empties itself

`SpinePrint` held its prints in an `NSCache` with neither limit set, so it grew until the system took
the lot under memory pressure and every spine was printed again. It now holds ten thousand spines or a
hundred megabytes of them, whichever binds first, costed by the bytes of each bitmap. At a spine's size
the memory is what binds.

`CoverImages` had the same shape of problem from the other end: three hundred covers counted and
nothing weighing them, which at the size covers are kept is well over a hundred megabytes of decoded
bitmaps. It's bounded by cost as well now.

## A spine outlives the cover it was printed from

The cache key carries whether the artwork was there to print, so a spine printed before its cover
arrived is asked for again once it has. That reads the wrong way round once the cover cache is smaller
than the library: a cover dropped to make room for another made the lookup miss, and the shelf drew a
bare spine and then drew it over again when the cover came back.

The spine printed on the artwork is looked for first now, whatever is in memory at the time. Only a
book whose spine has never been printed with its cover falls back to the bare one, which is what lets
the covers rotate freely while the spines stay put.

## Covers pulled off the disk rather than out of the books

`CoverCache.image(for:)` is memory, then disk, then the network. Anything working ahead of the reader
wants the first two and never the third, since a cover that has to be fetched has already lost the
race. `held(for:)` is that pair on its own, and both the press and the library's prefetching read
through it.

The library's collection view has a prefetch data source now, warming the covers of the cards about to
appear. One set at a time, the latest asked for: a fling asks for a hundred cards it goes straight
past, and the reader is only ever waiting on the last of them.

## What the press hadn't reached

A trace off the device put 4.8% of the main thread still in spine drawing, and 3.3% of that in one path:
a cover landing fires `onArtwork`, and the spine was reprinted there and then. It goes through the press
now like everything else, and `SpinePrint` has no way left to draw on the main actor at all. `image(of:)`
is gone; `standing(of:)` looks up what is filed and says whether it is the one wanted.

A bare print stands while the one carrying the artwork is pulled, so a cover landing no longer blinks. A
recycled view clears first, since it would otherwise hold up the book that stood there before it.

## A gap drew itself every time

`GapView.draw(_:)` was 6.5% of the main thread, and 7.9% of the stretch where the shelf was full of
volumes nobody holds. Two gradients, a plate, a stroke and a dynamic colour, on every layout pass.
`GapPrint` prints one instead, keyed by which side, how big it stands, which volume it is and which way
the room runs, which is the same handful of things `SpinePrint` and `MarkPrint` are keyed by.

## A card is handed to another author whole

Building books came to 12% of the main thread, counting the destroyers after them. A cell reused for
another author threw away every book on it and built the same number again, about twenty objects apiece.
`ShelfView` keeps them back now and stands the next author's books in them.

## A turn that set out from where it was going

A card's height was carried from `carrying.from` to `carrying.to`, and both were read after the new
contents had replaced the old, so they were the same figure. The height arrived in one frame while the
books turned inside a card that had already resized. The heights are read before the contents are
swapped now.

`AuthorCardView.height(across:)`, which interpolates through the shelf's own clock, turns out to be
called from the tests and nowhere else. They were exercising a path the app doesn't use, which is why
nothing caught it.

## Picking books out is gone

Two ways to hold books together was one too many. The checklist, the bar along the bottom, the ticks on
covers and cards, `isSelecting`, `selection` and the naming alert have all gone. The menu's two sheets
are the way now, and a book from a file is added from the profile beside Litres rather than from a
button over the shelf.

## The tabs, and the stacks under them

The bar didn't slide as the shelf was pulled up: it went in one step and came back in another, because
SwiftUI's `TabView` wasn't tracking the collection view inside it. A `UITabBarController` is, and
`tabBarMinimizeBehavior` is what was wanted all along.

Half of it wasn't enough. A tab bar controller holding hosting controllers fixed the sliding and broke
the hiding, because a pushed screen takes the tab bar with it and only a navigation controller can say
so. Each tab that opens a book is a `UINavigationController` now, its screen has dropped its own
`NavigationStack`, and a `Navigator` does the pushing.

Then the reader stopped rendering, thirteen tests at once. `WorkScreen` and `SeriesScreen` were still
pushing with `NavigationLink(value:)`, which does nothing whatever once the stack and its
`navigationDestination` are gone: no error, just a dead button. `Navigator` wasn't handing itself to
what it pushed either, so a converted work screen couldn't have pushed the reader in any case.

The zoom came out better than it went in. It used to grow out of an empty stand-in laid over the tapped
cover, since a zoom wanted a SwiftUI view and the shelf draws its own books. UIKit takes a view, so the
shelf hands over the board the artwork is on, and the stand-in, the namespace, the overlay and the frame
it was posed at have all gone.

## A book a view was holding from before

A `BookView` kept the tap and menu closures it was handed when it was made, and those capture the place,
which holds the book as it was then. A book read since stood there offering yesterday's menu.

## Signing lives in .env

The team and the profile were in `Local.xcconfig`, gitignored but a second place to keep local values.
`.env` is the first, so `Scripts/gen-signing.sh` writes the xcconfig out of it before each build. That
has to happen before xcodebuild rather than in a build phase: a project-level xcconfig is read when
build settings are evaluated, which is before any phase of the build runs.

Release signs with the development profile now, there being no distribution one, which is what puts an
optimised build on a device.

## A footnote marker on the wrong letters

A marker showed three letters of a word on the next line raised instead of `[2]`. `ChapterContent`
maps each marker from the text as it arrived to the text as it's set, and it mapped the hyphenated
setting from the bound one rather than from the source. Its marks had already moved past every word
joiner binding put in, and the second mapping moved them again by the same count. In the reported
paragraph that was fifteen joiners, exactly the distance to the letters that came out raised. Both
settings now place their markers from the source marks, and `Typography.version` went to 6 so prepared
chapters holding the old positions are set again.

## A novel set as one chapter took minutes to open

"Stuck at 50%" was a book whose second chapter never finished measuring. `BookHTML.blocks(in:)` looked
for the next `<img` from every paragraph to the end of the chapter, case-insensitively, so a chapter with
no picture left read the whole rest of itself once per paragraph. Several FB2 files set half a novel as
one section, up to 670 KB. Sampling the test process put 5,058 of 5,065 samples in `blocks(in:)`. It now
finds the next paragraph and the next picture once and keeps each until the cursor passes it. A
generated chapter of 4,000 paragraphs behind one picture took 93 s before and 0.3 s after.

`BackupBooksTests` found it. It imports every book a backup kept and measures each chapter the way the
reader's own pass does; see `Documentation/Testing.md`.

## Search left the shelf

The library's own field went. The search tab has a Library and Author.Today switch in its bar instead,
and its library results are the shelf itself, author cards and all, narrowed to what was typed. Both tabs
share one `LibraryScreen.Model`: a second one would load the library twice and fall out of step the
moment a book was marked read from a search. The search is a parameter of `groups(matching:)` and
`shelves(matching:)` rather than a field of the model, so typing in one tab doesn't filter the other.

Profile and the design catalogue went into navigation controllers like the other tabs, so every push
takes the tab bar with it the same way. Reader appearance splits into the form, which Profile pushes,
and the sheet the reader presents it in.

## Pulling the shelf to refresh

Three things went wrong at once. The collection view kept inside SwiftUI's top safe area, so the large
title growing under a pull moved the list itself on every frame while UIKit was moving its content: it
now reaches under both bars and only its insets change. A pull made while the launch reload was still
running returned at once and ended the spinner under the finger; a second reload now waits for the
first. And the screen and the shelf both read `isLoading`, so each toggle rebuilt every card
mid-gesture; both now read the shelf first and never look at `isLoading` while it has books on it.

## Setting a chapter on several cores

`BackupBooksTests` ran a backup of 517 files and 51 service books through the reader's own pass. All the
files read and each chapter with text got pages, but six chapters took more than five seconds to lay
out. All six are novels set as one or two sections, 416 to 1,174 pages each.

`ColumnComposer` now shares a chapter's paragraphs out between workers away from the main actor. An
`NSAttributedString` can't cross threads under Swift 6 without an unchecked conformance, so each worker
typesets its own copy from the same `Sendable` inputs and returns its lines as plain values, and `ChapterLayout`
builds a line's `CTLine` from them when a page first draws it. `PageImage` became `Sendable` along the
way, its fields being immutable.

The first cut ran 16 workers and got nothing. Sampling put three quarters of every worker's time in
`_os_unfair_lock_lock_slow` under `-[NSAttributeDictionary newWithKey:object:]`: UIFoundation uniques
attribute dictionaries under one process-wide lock, and setting a line's tracking a character at a time
took it about fifty times a line. Changing it a run at a time and then only at the spaces took the
waiting from 31,000 samples to 3,800. Past four workers the lock still wins, so there are four.

Largest chapter of each of the six, optimised build on the simulator:

| | serial | 16 workers | 4 workers |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 671 pages | 3.24 s | 1.61 s | 1.03 s |
| 709 pages | 1.89 s | 1.14 s | 0.78 s |
| 416 pages | 2.16 s | 1.17 s | 0.81 s |
| 1,043 pages | 3.08 s | 1.79 s | 1.28 s |
| 1,174 pages | 3.50 s | 1.42 s | 1.04 s |
| 1,155 pages | 3.38 s | 1.39 s | 0.96 s |

What's left is serial: about half a second deciding a chapter picture's shading pixel by pixel in
`BookImages`, and, on a chapter's first opening only, a second and a half preparing its text.

`Scripts/app.sh test -O` had been compiling without the optimiser: it handed the settings to `build`
alone. Every test run that asked for `-O` before the fix measured `-Onone` code.

## "Page 2 of 46" in a book of a thousand pages

The footer's total was the pages measured so far. Opening a book measures through the chapter after the
reader's, and only that pass refreshed the total: the pass that measures the rest behind the reader
never did, so the total stayed at the first two chapters. The rest now refreshes it after every chapter,
and until the pass is done the unmeasured chapters are guessed from their `textLength` at the rate the
measured ones ran.

## The design page

The catalogue stood wider than the screen and lost both edges, which is what made its first segment
look wider than the others. The row of marks had grown to twelve, about 414 points in a card with 342,
and an `HStack` doesn't wrap; it's a `FlowLayout` now.

The turning book never animated. SwiftUI steps an animation through a view's body but hands a
`UIViewRepresentable` only the value it ends on, and making the representable itself `Animatable`
changed nothing. An `Animatable` view around it does the stepping. The specimen turns at four times the
shelf's duration so the turn can be watched.

The picked-out specimen was a fixed rectangle behind the text. It picks from a point on one line to a
point on the next through `ChapterLayout.words(from:to:)` and paints `rects(of:)`, which is what the
reader does, so `BookTextView` takes a `BookTextPick` and a painting for it.

## Greys for light mode

Dark mode reads better than light, whose library is white cards on a pale screen. Surface.screen and
Surface.card now resolve their light colours through LightSurfaceTrial, seven candidate pairs picked
with -at-light-surface, while dark mode keeps the system's. Top, Search and Profile painted the system's
white behind their lists and now take the screen colour instead. Every pair was photographed on six
screens and on an invented library of 23 books, which -at-demo-library puts on the shelf without an
account. The trial goes once a pair is chosen.
