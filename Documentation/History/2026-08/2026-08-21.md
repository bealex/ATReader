# 2026-08-21 — sessions, offline, and the reader rebuilt around typography

A single long session, from "make the app work properly" to a reader that breaks its pages the way a
compositor would. Ten commits.

## 1. A session that outlives its token

Tokens last a day and nothing renewed them, so the app asked for a password about as often as a reader
opened it. Three changes, none of them large:

- The client retries a request once behind a refresh when the service rejects its token, and reports
  every token it adopts through a callback, which is what writes it to the keychain. Concurrent
  failures collapse into one refresh.
- The session renews a token within twelve hours of expiry, at launch and on every return to the
  foreground.
- Only a token the service actually rejects signs the reader out. A launch with no network restores
  the stored user instead, so the app opens on the library.

The last one is the point: "signed in" is a state the device owns, not something the network gets to
revoke by being unreachable.

**Untested:** with no test account to hand, this path is verified by construction and by the guest
suites. The 24-hour expiry has not been watched happen.

## 2. Everything the app shows, offline

The device now keeps one SQLite file under Application Support: books and their shelves, tables of
contents, chapter bodies, reading positions. Every screen paints from it before the service is asked
and keeps what it has when the service never answers.

Reading positions live there and nowhere else, because `reader/update-progress` accepts a position and
stores nothing. That was already known; what changed is that the device stops pretending the service
will remember.

The daily sweep grew accordingly: it walks the whole library rather than the first page of it, counts
the chapters this device has never seen, downloads them, and spends what's left of a per-sweep budget
backfilling. The background budget is a quarter of the foreground one, because a background window is
short and overrunning it gets the app killed.

Covers moved out of `Caches`, which the system may empty whenever the device runs low on space, into
Application Support, capped at 2000 with the least recently used dropped. They come back already
decoded, so the main thread never decodes one.

## 3. CoreText cannot hyphenate

The reader drew with CoreText, and justified Russian looked wrong: the space between *letters*
stretched to fill lines. The obvious fix, `hyphenationFactor`, does nothing to a `CTFramesetter`. It
is a TextKit setting.

The next idea was to hyphenate by hand: `CFStringGetHyphenationLocationBeforeIndex` has dictionaries
for both `ru` and `en`, so soft hyphens could be inserted before layout. CoreText does break a line at
a soft hyphen. It draws no hyphen when it does.

That was worth proving rather than assuming, so a standalone program set a hyphenated line, rendered
it, and read the glyphs back: 22 characters, 20 glyphs, a word split across two lines and no hyphen to
show for it. Strictly worse than a loose line.

TextKit hyphenates from the same dictionaries and draws the hyphen. A benchmark first, because the
worry was speed: 42,000 characters into 51 pages in about 85ms, and *fewer* pages with hyphenation on
than off, since better breaks pack lines tighter. The reader moved to `NSLayoutManager`.

The cost is that TextKit stays on the main actor. Pagination lays the column out in slices with a
yield between them, so the work is spread over frames rather than blocking a page turn.

**Lesson:** when a documented setting does nothing, check which framework owns it before working
around it. Two frameworks that both draw text do not both hyphenate.

## 4. Breaking pages the way a compositor would

Letting TextKit fill page-sized containers gives you no say in where a page breaks. Setting the chapter
as one column and cutting it into pages line by line does, and that is what the rules need:

- no hyphen at the foot of a page,
- no orphan, no widow,
- no heading without at least two lines of its chapter under it,
- and four lines on a page, which outranks all of the above.

Whether it worked was a counting question, not a looking question. A temporary print in the page
breaker reported violations per chapter, and the first numbers were 2-4 hyphens at a page foot and up
to 2 orphans per chapter: a break that moves back one or two lines lands on another hyphenated line
when the text has a run of them. At four lines of pull-back the numbers were 0 hyphens, 0 widows, 1
orphan in 87 pages, with pages still at a median of 15 lines. The instrumentation came out again.

The slack a pull-back leaves is spread between the lines of that page, up to 3pt per gap, and a gap can
be squeezed 0.75pt to pull one more line on. A page left half empty is a chapter ending and keeps its
ragged bottom.

Before any of that, `Typography` binds the words a line must not break between, and the two languages
disagree about which: Russian will not end a line on a short preposition nor start one with a dash,
English keeps an abbreviation with its name.

## 5. Chapters that run on

A chapter now starts on the page the one before it ended on, when six lines and a quarter of the page
are left. The layout takes a start offset and shortens its first page by exactly that much; the reader
draws such a page from two pieces, one per chapter.

The consequence is that layouts depend on each other and have to be prepared in order forwards. A
chapter opened from the contents starts a page of its own and is laid out again if the reader later
arrives from the chapter before.

The bug this exposed took a while to see: the composite page rendered only after something else forced
a redraw. The model's layout cache was `@ObservationIgnored`, so a neighbour landing changed no
property SwiftUI was watching. A page that can show a chapter which hasn't arrived yet has to observe the
arrival.

## 6. The page fills the screen

No navigation bar, no strip under the text. The book's title and the page number are drawn on the page
itself so a turn carries them along, and the controls are a tap in the middle away.

Two measurements settled two bugs. The text sat 113pt too low, because a page that ignores the safe
area is not the size its parent hands it: both the size and the device insets have to come from the
window. Then the controls sat a notch's worth too low, because an overlay *is* laid out inside the safe
area even when the view under it is not, so insetting the bar by hand applied the notch twice. First
was found by measuring rows of pixels in a screenshot, second by reading the button's frame out of
XCUITest.

The bar is the reader's own rather than the navigation stack's, which is what lets it fade rather than
slide. Its buttons are round Liquid Glass, 36pt across, measured against the system's own toolbar
button rather than guessed.

## 7. The turn

Paging had to feel like paper rather than like a slideshow:

- The incoming page eases in from the screen edge to meet the finger over 0.3s and is then held 20pt
  inside its own leading edge. Measured with the drag held: the page's edge sits at 0.34 of the width
  with the finger at 0.40.
- What lands a turn is the finger's travel, not how far the page has come, and a flick back cancels it
  however far it had got.
- The page behind draws back 5% and darkens as it is covered; the page in front carries the shadow.
- At the ends of the book the page gives through a rubber band where it used to sit still: 60.6pt of
  give under a 241pt pull, against 60.3 predicted, springing back on release.
- A turn in flight is dropped when the app leaves the screen.

Crossing a chapter break animates onto the neighbouring chapter's own page, so the chapter swaps under
the animation. Prepared layouts either side are what make that instant.

## 8. iOS 27 only

The deployment target moved from 18 to 27, which removed the availability dance around the glass style.
The device it deploys to runs 27.0; the other paired devices, on 26.5, can no longer install it.
