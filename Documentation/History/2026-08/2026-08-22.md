# 2026-08-22 — how a book presents itself

The library and the book page were showing everything they knew about a book, in the order the code
happened to compute it. This session was about what a reader actually looks at.

## 1. The library opens on what you are reading

The shelf filter defaulted to everything, so a library of two hundred books opened on two hundred
books. It now opens on the Reading shelf, and the filter widens it.

Books are grouped by author, and within an author by series. The grouping is a dictionary keyed by
(author, series); standalone titles come before an author's series, and books inside a series sort by
the service's `seriesOrder` before their title.

A long press moves a book between shelves or off them, which is the same `update-library-state` call
the book page already made.

## 2. Progress belongs on the cover

A linear bar under each row said the same thing three times over: a bar, a percentage, and a date.
It's now one ring on the cover itself, with the figure inside it, fixed at 30×30 whatever the cover's
size, because it's a badge rather than part of the artwork.

The "continue reading" strip lost its titles. A cover is the fastest thing to recognise, and the strip
is now sorted by when the *book* last changed rather than when the reader last opened it: the book with
a new chapter in it is the one worth picking up.

## 3. Pills

A book's facts (likes, whether it's finished, whether it's paid for, how far in you are, when it last
changed) were a mix of grey `Label`s and a page count derived by dividing characters by 1800. The count went. The rest are pills, tinted by what they say, with the glyph 3pt from its word
rather than a label's default gap.

They wrap, which SwiftUI has no stack for, so `FlowLayout` is a small `Layout` that fills a line and
starts another.

## 4. Disclosure chevrons

Every list row was a `NavigationLink`, which draws a chevron a book row doesn't need. A row is now a
button that appends to the stack's path; nothing else about navigation changed, and the UI tests that
tap cells never noticed.

## 5. The bar the system draws

The reader's bar had been hand-built to match a navigation bar, measured against one, and still read
as not quite right. It's now the navigation bar: `.toolbar(.hidden / .visible, for: .navigationBar)`
follows the same tap that used to fade a custom overlay in.

The reason the custom bar existed was that showing a real one would push the page down. That stopped
being true when the page started taking its size and insets from the window, so the change costs
nothing. The bar carries the page's colour, made visible explicitly, since the running head passes
under it.

## 6. The cover that blinked

Turning a page from the title page made the cover flash. `PageTurnView` puts the incoming page in a
different slot of its `ZStack`, so the view is rebuilt under a new identity, `@State` starts empty, and
the cover comes back through an `await` on the cache actor. One frame of placeholder, every turn.

Decoded covers now sit in a main-actor `NSCache` as well, so a view that is rebuilt draws its cover in
the first frame. The actor stays where covers are downloaded, downsampled and stored.

## 7. What the first look at it found

Reading it on a device turned up five things, all small:

- The figure in the ring is 20% smaller, and a finished book shows a tick instead of "100%".
- Book lists have no row separators. A cover and a title are their own boundary.
- The library orders by what was last read or last updated rather than by author name. Alphabetical
  order is only useful when you already know what you're looking for.
- The covers in the "continue reading" strip take the same long press as the rows.
- A refused shelf move now says what the service said, instead of "Couldn't update your library", and
  reloads the library so the shelf on screen is the one the service holds. The shelf is the service's
  own field: a book read to its end stays on Reading until something moves it.

## 8. Two more from the shelf

A series now runs latest book first, since the one with a chapter still arriving is the one being read.
Titles compare numerically, so a series the service gives no `seriesOrder` for lands in the same order
anyway.

Reading also drops a book once it's been read to the end. The shelf is still the service's field, and
the service leaves a finished book on it; the list stops showing what there is nothing left to read.
The number beside a shelf in the filter is counted through the same rule rather than taken from the
service's own totals, so it is the number of rows the shelf opens.

## 9. The shelf was the wrong idea

Read on a real library, the list was wrong: two books already read sat in it and nothing being read did.
The Reading shelf is the reader's own filing on the website, and this reader doesn't file. Every rule
built on top of it inherited that.

So the shelves are out of the library. A book's state comes from two facts the service does report
honestly, whether the author has written the last chapter and how much of the text has been read:

- **finished** — written to its end and read to its end. Both, or it isn't finished.
- **caught up** — read as far as it goes, with the author still writing. Read, but not finished.
- everything else is being read.

The list opens on what isn't finished, which is the set with something in it to open. The filter
switches to the finished ones or to all of them.

The words moved with the meaning. "Finished" used to be the pill for an author who had stopped writing,
which is why a book could read as finished while its reader was half way through it. That pill says
"Complete" now, and "Finished" says the reader is done.

What's left of the service's library state is the one thing it says without ambiguity: whether a book
is in the library. The book page's three shelf buttons became one, "Add to library", and a long press
in the list removes. The daily sweep follows the same rule as the list rather than the Reading shelf,
so it checks the books that can have an unseen chapter.

## 10. Read in two languages

Alignment is now one setting per language, Russian justified and English left-aligned by default. The
style carries both answers rather than one, because which language a chapter is in isn't known until
it has been parsed, and the typesetter already has the language in hand when it builds the paragraph
style.

Four smaller things from the same read-through:

- The reader's bar has a shadow. Painted the page's own colour with nothing under it, there was
  nothing to say where the bar ended and the page began. `toolbarBackground` takes a colour and no
  shadow, so `readerBarAppearance` sets an opaque `UINavigationBarAppearance` and a layer shadow on
  the enclosing `UINavigationController`, and puts back what it found on the way out.
- Only a book still being written carries a writing-status pill. "Complete" would sit on nearly every
  row saying nothing.
- The book page shows every tag, wrapped rather than scrolling sideways, and every chapter, with no
  expander.
- The page count went. It was characters divided by 1800, which is a guess dressed as a figure.

## 11. Hyphenation that earns its column

Justified Russian was leaving lines stretched letter by letter — one page had `к а  о д н о г о  и з
е е  в а г о н о в` across a full measure — which meant TextKit had not broken the word that followed.

The dictionary wasn't the limit: `CFStringGetHyphenationLocationBeforeIndex` offers four or five
positions in a long Russian word (`пре-ду-пре-ди-ла`, `пас-са-жир-ско-го`). What was limiting it was
how eagerly TextKit asks. Setting `hyphenationFactor = 1` on justified paragraphs moved exactly one
line on the test page and left the page count where it was.

Marking the breaks in the text itself is what worked. Every word goes through the dictionary and every
position it allows gets a soft hyphen, two letters in from either end. Same chapter, same style: 34
pages became 33, four hyphens on a page became seven, and the stretched line broke `«При-азовск»`
instead.

The two consequences were the interesting part:

- A soft hyphen every eight characters means the laid-out text no longer counts the same as the text
  the service sent, and the reading position is a character offset. It is converted at the layout's
  edge now, so a change of alignment can't move a reader's place.
- Marking a chapter costs about what laying it out costs — 134ms for 76,000 characters on the Mac —
  which is too much to repeat on every re-pagination. It happens in the detached task that already
  parses the chapter, once, and the layout picks the marked paragraphs or the plain ones by alignment.

## 12. Stopping counts as reading

The position was written 400ms after a page turn, which is right while a reader is turning pages and
wrong the moment they stop: an app on its way to the background never runs a task that is still
waiting, and neither does one being killed after it.

`flushPosition` gives the wait up. It writes the position at once and reports upstream whatever the
last coarse step sent, and the reader calls it when the scene stops being active and when the screen
goes away. The upstream half still lands nowhere, since the service stores nothing it is sent; the
write to `LocalStore` is the one that matters.

A UI test now reads three pages, puts the app away, kills it, comes back and expects the same page.
It doesn't pin the timing down — several seconds pass before the app is backgrounded, so the ordinary
debounce would have written by then too — but it holds the contract the change is there to keep.

## 13. A spinner needs something to stand on

The library's loading spinner was drawn straight onto the screen with no background, which reads as
something gone wrong rather than something being fetched. Five screens had the same pattern, and they
divide in two:

- Nothing behind them yet — the library, a book page, a chapter, the session being restored. A spinner
  that fills the screen and brings its own background.
- Rows already on screen — the chart being rebuilt, a search being re-run. Those keep their rows and
  put the spinner on a card over them.

## 14. What a row says, and what it shows

The pills were saying the wrong things in the wrong order:

- The state comes first now, because it is the thing a row is asked about. "Ongoing" for a book still
  being written, "Finished" for one written and read to its end, nothing for the ordinary case.
- "Paid" is gone. It sat on most rows and told a reader nothing they could act on. In its place a `$`
  appears only on a book that costs money and hasn't been bought, and the book page marks which
  chapters that money buys: the unreadable ones carry the same `$`, and the ones without it are free.
- "Caught up" is gone too. The ring already says it, and says it in the place the eye is looking.

The covers were cropped, which was a local mistake rather than the service's: a fixed 2:3 box filled
with an image of another shape cuts its edges off. They are fitted to their width now and keep their
own height. The ring's blue line is 0.2pt wider and inset by half its own width, so its outer edge
lands on the badge's edge and no backing shows around it, and a finished book's tick is large enough
to read at 30pt.

The "continue reading" strip is gone. Grouping is by series alone, since a series with two authors is
still one series and the author belongs on the row, and a series name sits closer to its books.

## 15. The contents as a checklist

A chapter list that says nothing about what has been read is a list of names. It now carries a mark per
chapter: a ring filled as far as the reader got, a tick once a chapter is behind them, an outline for
one they haven't opened. A paid chapter keeps its `$` instead, since there is no progress to show in a
chapter that can't be opened.

There is one position per book to work from, not one per chapter, so the states are arithmetic on the
chapter order: before the position is read, at it is part read, after it is unread. The percentage
comes from the offset against the chapter's own `textLength`, and the service's `lastChapterId` and
`lastChapterProgress` stand in on a device that has never opened the book.

The blurb is cut to five lines. Knowing whether to offer the control means knowing whether the text was
cut, which SwiftUI won't say: a hidden copy with no line limit is laid out at the same width, and being
taller than the visible one is the answer.

## 16. The price marker was guessing

`$` appeared on books the reader owns. The rule was `isPaid && isPurchased != true`, which treats a
field the service didn't send as a "no". Measured against the live service as a guest: the catalogue
carries no `isPurchased` at all, and `work/details` doesn't report one either. Everything paid looked
unbought.

The flag alone can't carry it, so the book page reads the answer off what is locked: a sold book with a
chapter closed to this reader has not been bought. That works for a guest, and for a reader whose
purchase the service never mentions. Lists have no contents to go on and now show the marker only when
the service positively says a sold book is unbought, which is the one case where being sure is
possible.

## 17. Marking a book read

A long press now offers "Mark as read", which fills the ring and fills the book page's chapter marks
with it, by putting the position at the end of the last chapter the book has. If the device has no
contents for that book, it fetches them first.

Making it stick took more than writing a number. The library's progress came from the service, and a
refresh would have wiped the mark within seconds. The service stores no progress this app sends, so
its figure is only ever as fresh as the last time the reader read somewhere else. `LocalStore` now
keeps the device's own figure beside each book: the reader writes it as it turns pages, "mark as read"
sets it to one, and a refresh raises it but never lowers it. That also fixes something older than this
change, since a book read only in this app used to show whatever progress the website last knew.

## 18. Marking read didn't take

The mark filled the ring and then lost it. The store was doing its part — the upsert keeps the device's
figure against a service refresh reporting less, checked against SQLite directly — but the library
never read it back. `reload` painted the entries as they arrived from the service, and the service
carries no progress this app made, so the next refresh put the old number back.

The list is read back out of the store after a refresh now, which is what the store was for. The
service's copy goes in, the device's own knowledge comes out.

## 19. An overlay that hid a loaded library

"Loading your library" stayed on screen. Nothing was stuck: the library was behind it the whole time.

`showStoredLibrary` paints what the device has before the service is asked, but the overlay's condition
was `isLoading && !hasLoaded`, and `hasLoaded` only turns true when the network answers. While the
spinner had no background this read as a spinner floating over the list. Giving it one turned that into
a screen with the library hidden behind it for as long as the service took.

The rule is the one the component was written for: cover a screen only while there is nothing to cover.
The library shows it while `works` is empty, the book page while it has no summary to draw. `reload`
also clears its loading flag through a `defer` now, so no path can leave the flag standing and lock the
guard at the top of it for good.

## 20. Two bugs in one call, and what the service really keeps

Removing a book failed with the service's own "что-то пошло не так". The log said it plainly once
failed requests were logged: `POST /v1/account/update-library-state → 500 InternalServerError`.

This machine can't reach `api.author.today` at all — not from the shell, not from the fetch tool — but
the simulator can, so a throwaway test pulled the service's OpenAPI document through it. The field is
`workIds`. This client had been sending `ids`, so the service read its own field as null and threw.
That is both failures at once: the shelf move that failed days earlier and the removal now. The
project's own API notes had recorded the wrong shape, which is where the implementation came from.

Then the harder question: is reading position really local-only? The service's own site asks "continue
where you left off", so something stores it. It does:

- `GET /v1/account/reading-progress` returns real positions — 20 entries for a live account, chapter
  ids and timestamps months apart, written by the website's reader. The app adopts them now.
- Progress is a **percentage**, not a fraction. Values come back as `93.022125` and `96.119712`. This
  client clamped its writes to `0…1`, which claimed half a percent whatever the reader had done.
- Writes still vanish. Tested with a value the service had not seen, in percent, with a `sessionId`
  from a live `reader/start`, spaced past the rate limit: the call succeeds and nothing moves.
- That endpoint allows one call a second per IP. Closer than that reads as a timeout, which is how the
  earlier attempts looked.

So the position this device makes stays here, and the position the service holds is read and adopted.
What is left unexplored is `sync-reading-stats`, which throws a server-side `NullReferenceException`
from the same `ReverseString` helper the chapter key derivation uses: it wants a derived field the
Swagger doesn't document, and that is where writing would have to start.

## Verified

Fifteen catalogue UI tests pass against the live service, including the reader's controls and page
turns. Read out of XCUITest, the reader's bar measures the same as the book page's: origin (0, 62),
402×54, back button 44×44 at x=16. That is where the system puts a navigation bar, so there is nothing
left to match by hand. Screenshots taken from a scratch XCUITest confirmed the bar's buttons, the pills and the row
without a chevron; the library screens need an account and were not seen running.
